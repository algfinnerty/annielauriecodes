UX Research Methods: An Overview

Designing Applications is HARD.

The only way to know you are making good design decisions is to test with users and stakeholders. The right feedback at the right time can validate your assumptions, correct oversights, and reveal new opportunities to delight your users.

This reference guide compares several common research methods to help you select the most appropriate strategies quickly.

For each method you will find:

  • The relative cost and effort
  • The time and resources required, and
  • The types of results you can expect.

While some methods are passive, others will need a designated researcher to write questions, conduct interviews, or even go out into the field. You might take on the role of researcher, but another member of your team or a third party might conduct studies on your behalf.

Types of Research Methods

Methods are categorized as qualitative (producing numeric or statistical results) and/or quantitative (producing personal or narrative results). User research experts recommend using multiple research methods that provide both types of results.

Luckily many methods combine nicely with others, letting you gather multiple types of data in the same research session.

Qualitative Methods

Qualitative research methods produce results that are not numbers - narratives, interviews, and recordings.

They provide greater detail, personal viewpoints, and sometimes surprising results. This makes qualitative results harder to analyze.

But the results provide deeper insight into your respondents. Expect to spend more time analyzing qualitative results by grouping responses and identifying themes.

Interview

Results Type: Narrative
What: Directed conversation with a single user or stakeholder
Why: Interviews give a deep look into respondents and generate feedback that is honest, specific, and difficult to obtain from other methods.
When: During initial discovery or product redesign
Testing concepts and prototypes
As a follow-up to other research methods
Where: In-Person or Remote
Required: 3-5 interview subjects
Focused interview questions
Two interviewers (one to ask questions and one to take notes)
How: Develop rapport with respondent. Try to do some background research about them, if possible. Interviewers also need to know the application, features, or changes the interview will focus on. Interviewers should be prepared to explore new topics brought up by the subject, while trying to keep them focused on specific research areas.
Cost: Varies. Busy respondents may require monetary incentives.
Time: Medium
Risks: Relying on only a few interviews may skew results. Respondents should be diverse, chosen carefully, and encouraged to speak candidly.

Focus Groups

Results Type: Audio recordings and notes
What: Facilitated interview with a group of users or stakeholders
Why: Focus groups produce lots of feedback quickly. Results will lack details compared to one-on-one interviews but will include more perspectives. Focus groups reveal social dimensions and how respondents talk about a product.
When: During initial discovery to clarify research questions
Testing concepts and prototypes
To understand about how users talk about a product
Where: In-Person or Remote
Required: 8-10 respondents per focus group
Prepared questions
A moderator or facilitator
How: A moderator or facilitator should keep the group on task and focused on relevant questions. Focus groups tend to work better when they are limited in time and scope.
Cost: Medium. A monetary incentive (or at least free coffee) will help recruit more respondents.
Time: Medium
Risks: Groups can be difficult to keep on topic and strong personalities may sway opinions. A professional facilitator will produce the best results but will be more expensive.

Field Studies

Results Type: Video and/or notes
What: Observing how respondents perform tasks in the real world
Why: Tasks can be difficult for respondents to describe during interviews. Field studies show what people do - not what they say they do. Observing respondents in their everyday environments reveals details that are difficult to discover with other research methods.
When: See tasks performed in the real world
Gain detailed insight into a user
Find pain points, gaps, and opportunities
Where: In-Person, with the user performing a task
Required: One or more respondents who perform a task
Recording equipment
One or more researchers
How: One or more researchers go in person to a respondent's location and observe them performing tasks in real life. These tasks may or may not involve using your application directly. Researchers should take detailed notes and record video of the respondent's workflow if possible. Field studies are frequently combined with one-on-one interviews.
Cost: Varies
Time: Medium
Risks: Focusing on a limited number of users may skew results. Field studies may also raise questions that only more research can answer.

Ethnographic Research

Results Type: Video recordings, audio recordings and/or notes
What: Observing a user's entire day in their normal environment.
Why: Ethnographic research gives the most detailed picture of a respondent's way of life. This may or may not involve use of your application, or even tasks related to it - ethnography is interested in everything about a respondent. This method also gives deep insights into cross-cultural and accessibility issues that may be difficult to uncover with other methods.
When: Gain detailed insight into a respondent
Find pain points, gaps, and opportunities
Understand the full context of a product in daily life
Where: In-Person
Required: One or more respondents willing to participate
Recording equipment
One or more researchers
How: Closely observe a single respondent for their entire day. Researchers take notes and record audio and video where possible. You can include other research methods.
Cost: Medium. Providing large incentives for ethnographic research may skew the results.
Time: Very High
Risks: Focusing on a limited number of users may skew results. Researchers should choose ethnographic subjects carefully to make sure they are good candidates.

Diary Studies

Results Type: Diary entries
What: Asking respondents to write brief diary entries, usually daily. Entries may be highly structured or more open-ended.
Why: Show changes in user views and behaviors over time.
When: Defining user habits
Uncovering attitudes and feelings
Generating longitudinal data about how user's feelings change over time
Where: Passive
Required: A screener survey to identify the best respondents
Instructions for recording diary entries
A template or form to record entries
Grouping and theming responses
How: Recruit at least five respondents who will commit to creating diary entries. Provide clear expectations and examples of the kinds of diary entries you expect to receive but be careful not to influence their responses.
Cost: Medium. Incentives may increase the quality of responses or make recruitment easier.
Time: Medium. Analysis time will increase with the number of responses.
Risks: Low control over the results. Not a good method for investigating specific questions.

Usability Testing

Results Type: Notes and video
What: Observing a user's interaction with an application or prototype.
Why: Seeing a user interact directly with an application can validate design decisions and reveal unexpected pain points.
When: Clarify research questions during initial discovery
Find pain points, gaps, and opportunities
Test new concepts and prototypes
Where: In-Person or Remote
Required: A working product or prototype
Recording equipment
A facilitator or observer (optional)
How: Design tests to be specific to the research questions you are asking. Researchers can observe in case respondents get stuck. If unobserved, respondents have to figure things out on their own. There are advanced recording options like mouse tracking and eye tracking, but video recording or screen sharing can also work.
Cost: Varies
Time: High
Risks: Researchers must be careful not to influence respondents during observed tests. Unobserved tests run the risk of users getting stuck and not fully using all the features.

Quantitative Methods

Quantitative research methods produce numeric results that are easy to analyze and present using charts and diagrams. They show user behavior on a large scale and help make high-level decisions backed up by data.

Quantitative methods are also easily repeatable. This is great for long-term research and understanding the impact of product changes.

Heatmaps

Results Type: Images
What: A map showing the areas of a page where a user or group of users focus their attention.
Why: Heatmaps show where and how users spend time in an application. This includes page areas with high mouse activity. Heatmaps can also display touch behavior, eye tracking, or other input methods. Shows both successful and unsuccessful interactions, how users move through an application, when they get stuck, and when they try to interact with elements that are not interactive.
When: After several months of user interaction
Testing new features and design changes
Where: Passive
Required: An analytics tool
Development time (low)
How: Heatmaps are generated by adding small snippets of code from a third-party application to an existing application. After that, heatmaps will be generated automatically.
Cost: Incredibly low. Free on Hotjar, bundled with most paid analytics tools.
Time: Very low. After initial setup, heatmaps are generated automatically and only require time for analysis.
Risks: Accurate heatmaps take time and many diverse users to gather. Analyze heatmaps only after an appropriate amount of data has been collected.

Surveys

Results Type: Statistics
What: Sending users a brief series of targeted questions.
Why: Surveys are easy to create and provide focused results for easy analysis. You can gather user demographics, pose targeted questions, and find data to back up your decisions. They are inexpensive, repeatable, and highly scalable.
When: Evaluating established applications
Ranking or rating new or desired features
Getting data for business decisions
Where: Passive
Required: A screener survey to find the best respondents
A large group of participants; use a sample size calculator to find out how many you need.
A questionnaire
A way to collect results
Analysis time (low)
How: Use both closed-ended and open-ended questions. Closed-ended questions provide user options and are easier to analyze. Open-ended questions produce results that need to be grouped and themed.
Cost: Very low
Time: Medium. Receiving results may also be a slow process.
Risks: It is easy to introduce bias into survey questions unintentionally. Make sure to word questions as neutrally as possible to ensure accurate results.

Net Promoter Score (NPS)

Results Type: Statistics
What: A one-question survey asking users if they would recommend your application.
Why: NPS is a key marketing metric. It provides a very concise view into how users feel about a product and is essential for showing business value.
When: Any time after product launch
After a user has made a purchase or completed a task
Where: Passive
Required: A way to collect results
Development time (low)
How: One-question NPS surveys pop up after a user completes a task. They can be included in longer surveys, but one-question surveys have higher completion rates. There are several free and paid tools to automate NPS surveys and calculate metrics for the results.
Cost: Varies
Time: Very low
Risks: None

A/B Testing

Results Type: Statistics
What: Present two versions (A and B) of a feature to different user groups, then compare their responses using another user research method.
Why: The best way to measure the response to new versions or changes in your product. It reduces the risk of changes by testing a subset of users before new features are released.
When: Before a new feature release
Part of the redesign process
Where: Remote
Required: A survey
A way to collect results
Development time (low)
How: Most A/B testing is set up in a deployment pipeline. Identify the features you want to A/B test, then work with your developers to develop a testing strategy. Traffic to the A/B versions does not need to be equally divided. It might even be based on user characteristics like permissions or location. Try to use only two versions of an application at a time to keep comparisons simpler.
Cost: Varies
Time: Medium
Risks: Not valuable unless a second research method is employed. A/B testing should only be used to validate decisions that have already been backed up by earlier research.

Analytics/Logging

Results Type: Statistics
What: Recording the actions a user takes on a webpage or computer program.
Why: Analytics and logging provide the most reliable data on a user's actions and an application's performance. They are well suited to data mining and advanced analytic techniques which can reveal surprising but provable conclusions. These methods are also valuable for other purposes like showing business value, resolving customer service issues, and fixing bugs in software.
When: Any time
Where: Passive
Required: Developer time
Analysis time
How: Developers add custom code to track specific user interactions like page views or clicks and send them to an analytics application. This application will aggregate events, provide breakdowns by demographic, and even show the 'path' or 'funnel' of a user's journey through an application.
Cost: Free through Google Analytics, but paid alternatives may offer better analysis tools.
Time: Low
Risks: Unlike heatmaps, analytics and logging do not always show where a user gets stuck - only when they give up. Bots and web crawlers may contaminate data collected from public webpages. Depending on the type of data collected, there may be additional privacy concerns. It is always better to anonymize logging and analytics data.

Card Sorts

Results Type: Statistics
What: Asking users to sort items into categories, either alone or with a group. Card sorts are a great way to validate site hierarchies and navigation.
Why: Card sorts show how users naturally group concepts into categories. They also help uncover the existing mental models of your users to help shape the information architecture of your application.
When: Clarify information architecture
Find pain points especially in application navigation
Test new concepts and design changes
Where: In-Person or Remote
Required: 15 or more respondents
A list of items for respondent to sort
A list of categories (optional)
Physical or digital cards
A similarity matrix to interpret the results.
How: Respondents sort topics (on physical or digital cards) into preexisting categories (closed sorting) or categories they decide on (open sorting), or a mix of both (hybrid sorting). Card sorts can be moderated or unmoderated, and they may involve one respondent or many working as a team.
Cost: Medium
Time: High
Risks: Results from card sorting are difficult to analyze. Closed sorts run the risk of missing important categories. Open sorts may lead to many new categories and no longer fit with the business model.

SEO Metrics

Results Type: Statistics and Keywords
What: If your product is a public website, SEO metrics reveal valuable insights about how search engines view your content and its domain(s).
Why: If you are trying to attract customers to your site, understanding how it appears in search engines is essential.
When: After several months of user interaction
Regular monitoring
Testing new content and performing SEO experiments
Where: Passive
Required: Marketing/Researcher time
How: A variety of free and paid tools exist to help you analyze different SEO Metrics including your domain authority, backlinks, and associated keywords. Checking the SERPs (Search Engine Result Pages) for your site and keywords regularly is important - it shows how easy it is for people to find your application!
Cost: Varies
Time: Low, but SEO metrics should be frequently checked.
Risks: None
