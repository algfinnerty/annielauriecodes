Designing a Collaborative AI Recipe Experience

Exploring how an AI-assisted recipe concept could help people cook with ingredients they have while keeping users in control of recommendations and substitutions.

Role
UX/UI Designer for AI
Focus
Human-AI interaction, interaction design, wireframing, responsible AI
Project type
Stanford University UX/UI Design for AI capstone concept

Overview

Recipe recommendations involve more uncertainty than a typical search experience. I designed RecipeEasy, a concept for an AI-assisted recipe application that helps people create meals using ingredients they already have.

Rather than functioning as a conventional recipe search engine, RecipeEasy was designed as a collaborative cooking tool. It would remember a user’s preferences and limitations, suggest recipes and substitutions, and invite the user to refine its recommendations.

The project included interaction design, wireframes, preference and feedback flows, and an ethics review. Because it was a course concept rather than a built product, this case study focuses on the design decisions, interaction model, and responsible-AI questions I explored.

The challenge

Users may have firm dietary requirements, flexible preferences, limited ingredients, nutritional goals, or different needs within the same household. A recommendation that ignores those distinctions can be inconvenient—or, in some cases, inappropriate for the user.

The design therefore needed to answer several questions:

  • When should the system act automatically?
  • When should it ask the user for clarification?
  • How could users distinguish strict requirements from preferences?
  • How should the interface explain or qualify an AI-generated substitution?
  • How could the system learn without making users feel that they had lost control?
  • How could it support experimentation while communicating uncertainty?

The central UX challenge was not simply producing a relevant recipe. It was helping users confidently evaluate and shape the recommendation.

Design principle: collaboration over automation

I based the experience on collaboration rather than full automation.

RecipeEasy would make suggestions, summarize relevant information, and remember settings selected by the user. However, it would regularly ask for feedback and present recommendations for verification instead of silently making every decision.

Automation would be reserved for explicit, persistent requirements. For example, if a user had instructed the application to display only gluten-free recipes, the system should enforce that requirement consistently.

Other preferences would remain adjustable. A dietary consideration might be mandatory for one household but only relevant to some meals in another. To reflect that variation, I envisioned preferences as flexible controls rather than a collection of simple on-or-off settings.

This approach would allow the application to distinguish among:

  • Nonnegotiable dietary limitations
  • Long-term household preferences
  • Temporary goals for a particular meal
  • Ingredients a user wants to use or avoid
  • Areas where the user is open to experimentation

Designing for trust

My working premise was that users are more likely to trust an AI system when they can understand, verify, and influence its behavior.

RecipeEasy would feel less like submitting a query and more like working with a personal chef who already understands the household’s general needs but still asks what matters for tonight’s meal.

To support that relationship, I designed the concept around repeated opportunities for user input. Users could review ingredients, assess substitutions, adjust recipe goals, and refine instructions during the cooking process.

The interface needed to communicate:

  • Which preferences influenced a recommendation
  • Whether a suggestion respected the user’s stated requirements
  • Why an ingredient substitution might work
  • What other users reported about similar adjustments
  • Where the system was uncertain
  • How the user could correct or refine the result

The goal was not to make the AI appear infallible. It was to make the interaction understandable enough for users to decide when a recommendation was useful.

Interaction model

I created wireframes for several key points in the experience.

Home

The home experience introduced the primary task: tell the application what ingredients are available and what kind of meal the user wants to create.

It also provided access to persistent preferences while keeping meal-specific goals separate. This distinction allowed users to retain important long-term settings without forcing every cooking session into the same pattern.

Ingredient list

The ingredient-list experience allowed users to review what the system believed was available.

Because the quality of a recommendation depends on the input, this screen created an explicit verification point. Users could add, remove, or clarify ingredients before relying on the generated recipe.

Ingredient swaps

The swap experience explored how AI-supported substitutions could be presented without implying that every replacement was equivalent.

Instead of automatically changing an ingredient, the system could offer alternatives, explain why they might work, summarize relevant feedback, and let the user make the final selection.

This was especially important when substitutions affected dietary requirements, flavor, texture, cooking technique, or nutritional goals.

Procedure

The procedure experience extended collaboration into the cooking process.

Rather than presenting generated instructions as a fixed result, it allowed the user to review steps, request clarification, and respond when an ingredient or technique did not work as expected. These interactions could help the user complete the current recipe while generating useful feedback for future recommendations.

Feedback as part of the experience

Feedback from my capstone review reinforced the importance of designing an ongoing learning loop.

RecipeEasy would need to ask users:

  • Which recipes they liked
  • Where they needed additional help
  • Which substitutions worked
  • Which substitutions failed
  • What they changed during preparation
  • Whether the result met their dietary and household needs

This feedback would improve more than personalized recommendations. Aggregated carefully and responsibly, it could help identify patterns in successful ingredient combinations and modifications.

However, gathering more data would not automatically make the experience better. Feedback prompts would need to be timely, lightweight, transparent, and useful to the person providing the information—not simply a method of extracting data after every interaction.

A multi-model approach

Rather than relying on one general-purpose model to produce the entire experience, I proposed dividing the work among smaller, task-focused systems.

Individual models or services could address questions such as:

  • What ingredients could substitute for a missing item?
  • How can user feedback about a substitution be summarized?
  • Which highly rated recipes satisfy a specific combination of dietary requirements?
  • Does a suggested recipe conflict with a strict user preference?
  • What clarification should the system request before making a recommendation?

This approach could make individual interactions easier to evaluate and constrain. It would also create opportunities to apply different data sources, validation methods, and interface treatments to different types of recommendations.

The application would still need orchestration across those systems, but users would encounter a series of focused decisions rather than one opaque answer presented as authoritative.

Ethics review

As part of the project, I examined the ethical risks associated with an AI system providing personalized food recommendations.

  • Dietary safety: The interface should not blur the distinction between a preference and a restriction.
  • User control: Users should be able to inspect, correct, or remove remembered information.
  • Transparency: Recommendations should communicate their basis and limitations without overstating certainty.
  • Privacy: Dietary information and household preferences can be sensitive and should be collected only when necessary.
  • Bias and accessibility: Recommendations should not assume that users share the same budget, equipment, cooking ability, food access, or cultural expectations.
  • Feedback quality: Popular substitutions are not automatically appropriate for every person or recipe.
  • Accountability: The product should not frame generated nutritional or dietary guidance as professional advice.

The ethics review influenced the interaction design by reinforcing the need for verification, adjustable preferences, visible uncertainty, and limits on automatic decision-making.

Outcome

RecipeEasy remained a design concept and was not developed or released. I therefore do not claim product adoption, usability improvements, or model performance.

The completed project included:

  • A human-AI interaction concept centered on collaboration
  • User flows for recipe creation and refinement
  • Wireframes for the home, ingredient list, substitution, and procedure experiences
  • A preference model separating firm requirements from flexible goals
  • A feedback strategy for improving recommendations
  • A proposed multi-model system for focused AI tasks
  • An ethics review of safety, privacy, transparency, bias, and user control

The project demonstrates my ability to:

  • Design interactions around AI uncertainty
  • Balance automation with meaningful user control
  • Translate an AI concept into concrete interface flows
  • Incorporate responsible-AI questions into product design
  • Design preference and feedback systems for complex user needs
  • Communicate technical possibilities without treating AI as the product’s primary value

Reflection

The most important lesson from this project was that an AI experience should not be evaluated only by the quality of its first answer.

For RecipeEasy, the stronger design question was whether users could understand, challenge, and improve that answer. Cooking is personal, contextual, and often improvisational. A useful AI tool should support that human process rather than attempt to replace it.

RecipeEasy was therefore designed not as an automated recipe generator, but as a system that could help people experiment, learn, and make informed choices with greater confidence.
